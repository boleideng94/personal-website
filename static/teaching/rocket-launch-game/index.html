Momentum, carried away.

Here q ≥ 0 is the combined fuel-and-oxygen mass ejection rate. The rocket loses mass; exhaust leaves opposite the commanded thrust direction n̂. Gravity is an external force, so rocket + exhaust momentum is not constant in Earth's field.

ṁ = −q
T = q vₑ
vₑ = Isp · g₀
r̈ = −μr/|r|³ + (qvₑ/m)n̂

In the Earth frame, the idealized exhaust velocity is vrocket − vₑ n̂. A constant exhaust speed relative to the rocket is not a constant exhaust velocity relative to Earth.

High is not the same as in orbit.

We compute the engine-off osculating orbit from position and velocity. Success requires a bound ellipse with its entire orbit above your safe altitude, followed by 30 seconds with no thrust. A vertical launch into space can still fall straight back.

Δvideal = vₑ ln(m₀/mdry+payload)
vcircular = √(μ/r)

The optional target challenge requires both perigee and apogee within ±max(5 km, 5% of target altitude). A no-drag safe altitude is a classroom rule, not an atmospheric-lifetime prediction. After certification, continue coasting to watch the orbit.

What this model leaves out.

A nonrotating, spherical Earth; 2D translation; no drag, atmosphere, staging, Moon/Sun gravity, tank-mass scaling or rotational dynamics. Thrust direction changes instantly. The launch pad holds the rocket until its upward thrust exceeds its weight. Fuel burned on the pad still costs money.

Isp and mixture ratios are fixed teaching presets: 330 s / 2.6 (RP-1), 370 s / 3.5 (methane), and 450 s / 6 (hydrogen). Exhaust is an effective velocity; nozzle and ambient-pressure effects are absorbed into Isp. Tank/exhaust graphics are schematic, not a combustion simulation.

FLIGHT SCHOOL / 90 SECONDS

Altitude is the beginning.
Sideways speed makes an orbit.

Your rocket starts at rest on a nonrotating Earth. Choose its propellant, budget its mass, and steer a continuous, finite-duration burn.

1. Fill the glass rocket.

The fraction is propellant ÷ total launch mass, including payload. A 1 kg empty vehicle + 1 kg payload at 95% needs 38 kg of propellant, not 1.9 kg. Fuel and oxygen are consumed together.

2. Lift off, then turn.

Start pointed up with enough throttle to overcome gravity. Gradually tip toward 90° to build sideways velocity. Use the dial or drag from the rocket. “Prograde” follows your velocity; “Horizon” follows the local tangent.

3. Get the entire path above Earth.

The dashed line predicts your engine-off path. Perigee is its lowest point. Raising apogee alone is not enough: watch perigee, then cut the engine with X and coast for 30 simulated seconds.

4. Retry for a lower cost.

The score is the cost of all propellant purchased per kg of payload, not just what you burned. Try a lighter load or another fuel. Use the target goal to compare the same destination.

The guided launch uses exactly the same finite-thrust physics. It is a demonstration, not an optimal strategy or a ranked flight. It works with the default 95% load and 200 km target for all three propellants; unusual designs can run out of fuel.
